
## Scenario

### “Mr Tom MacDonald is a 59-year-old man admitted via the Emergency Department (ED) following a fall at work.”

- **Meaning:** this is an **acute presentation**, but the cause may be more than “just a fall.” His age puts him in a group where **fractures from low-energy events can be pathological** (e.g., cancer, osteoporosis).
    
- **Discussion prompts:**
    
    - Was it a true mechanical fall, or did pain/weakness cause collapse?
        
    - Any red flags before the fall (weeks of pain, weight loss, fatigue)?
        

### “He is complaining of acute, severe pain above his left knee.”

- **Meaning:** pain “above the knee” = think **distal femur** as well as knee joint. Severe pain at that location after minimal trauma strongly suggests **fracture** (possibly pathological).
    
- **Discussion prompts:**
    
    - Precisely where is maximal tenderness? Any deformity?
        
    - Is the pain “bone pain” (deep, constant) vs joint pain?
        

---

## Event

### “Mr MacDonald works in a factory.”

- **Meaning:** gives **functional baseline** and big implications for rehab/discharge—manual work, mobility needs, income pressure.
    
- **Discussion prompts:**
    
    - Will he be able to return to work? Any safeguarding/occupational support needed?
        

### “He apparently had a very minor twist of his leg while at work and fell to the ground in acute pain.”

- **Meaning (huge clue):** **minor mechanism → major injury** is a classic **pathological fracture** pattern. A normal femur usually doesn’t fracture from a tiny twist unless the bone is weak.
    
- **Discussion prompts:**
    
    - What makes bones “weak”? (metastasis, primary bone tumour, osteoporosis, infection)
        
    - Was there **pain before** the twist (yes—next sentence confirms)?
        

### “He is unable to weight-bear.”

- **Meaning:** raises urgency: can’t weight bear + severe pain = treat as **fracture until proven otherwise**.
    
- **Discussion prompts:**
    
    - Any neurovascular compromise?
        
    - Immediate immobilisation + analgesia needed.
        

### “He gives a six-week history of progressive pain in his left knee which has not been controlled with simple analgesia.”

- **Meaning (another huge clue):** a **prodrome** of progressive pain suggests an underlying process (tumour/infection/stress fracture) rather than a simple acute sprain. Not responding to paracetamol supports something more serious.
    
- **Discussion prompts:**
    
    - Night pain? Rest pain? Systemic symptoms (weight loss, cough)?
        
    - Any prior GP visits? Any imaging already?
        

---

## Past Medical History

### “Hypertension, myocardial infarction aged 52, followed by successful primary coronary intervention (PCI).”

- **Meaning:** important for **peri-operative risk**, anaesthetic planning, and meds interactions. Also suggests significant vascular disease history.
    
- **Discussion prompts:**
    
    - Is he on antiplatelets? (yes—next section)
        
    - Any chest pain, breathlessness now?
        

---

## Medication

### “Aspirin, bisoprolol, ramipril and atorvastatin daily.”

- **Meaning:** confirms secondary prevention after MI. Aspirin matters for **bleeding risk** if surgery is needed (and it likely will be). Beta-blocker affects heart rate interpretation.
    
- **Discussion prompts:**
    
    - Do we stop aspirin before surgery? Depends on urgency/type (orthopaedics will decide).
        
    - Ramipril considerations peri-op (often held day of surgery).
        

### “OTC Paracetamol 1 gram 4 times a day.”

- **Meaning:** he’s already taking **full-dose paracetamol** and still in significant pain → strengthens “this isn’t simple.” Also safety check: 4 g/day is max typical adult dose.
    
- **Discussion prompts:**
    
    - Any liver disease/alcohol excess? (he’s a “social drinker”)
        
    - Any additional OTC meds (ibuprofen, codeine)?

## Social History

### “He smoked at least 30 cigarettes a day from 16 to 52 years of age, when he gave up.”

- **Meaning:** very strong risk factor for **lung cancer** → later becomes extremely relevant when a chest X-ray shows a hilar lesion and biopsy shows adenocarcinoma.
    
- **Discussion prompts:**
    
    - Ask about cough, haemoptysis, weight loss, breathlessness.
        

### “He is a ‘social’ drinker and is married with 4 children and 6 grandchildren.”

- **Meaning:** social support network exists (good for recovery), but also big psychological impact if diagnosis is cancer/advanced disease.
    
- **Discussion prompts:**
    
    - Who should be involved in updates/decisions (with consent)?
        
    - What are his goals/values?
        

### “His 57-year-old wife works part time in the local Tesco store.”

- **Meaning:** potential **financial vulnerability** if he can’t work; affects discharge planning and support services.

## Examination

### “The patient is pale and sweating. GCS = 15.”

- **Meaning:** pale/sweaty = sympathetic stress (often **pain**, sometimes shock). GCS 15 means he’s alert and protecting airway.
    
- **Discussion prompts:**
    
    - Check Hb, look for bleeding (femur fractures can bleed a lot).
        
    - Pain control urgently.
        

### “Breathing – Respiratory rate 20 breaths/min, SpO2 98% (FiO2 = 0.21), good air entry on both sides, no added sounds.”

- **Meaning:** respiratory status currently stable. RR 20 could be mild pain/anxiety response. Normal sats doesn’t exclude cancer, but no acute chest issue evident right now.
    
- **Discussion prompts:**
    
    - Still consider PE/pneumonia if history suggests, but here it’s reassuring.
        

### “Pulse: 105 beats/min, BP 130/85 mmHg.”

- **Meaning:** tachycardia could be pain/stress. BP stable. In a femoral fracture you still watch for occult blood loss and deterioration.
    

### “The left leg is swollen above the knee and extremely tender to touch.”

- **Meaning:** local swelling + severe tenderness above knee = distal femur fracture/lesion until proven otherwise. Swelling can also be from bleeding.
    
- **Discussion prompts:**
    
    - Check compartments, skin integrity, deformity.
        
    - Move the limb as little as possible (stabilise early).
        

### “All foot pulses are present.”

- **Meaning:** reassuring—no obvious acute arterial occlusion distally. Still document cap refill, sensation, motor function.
    

### “A peripheral venous catheter/cannula (PVC) is inserted. He is given opioid analgesia through the PVC.”

- **Meaning:** correct ED priorities: **analgesia** + IV access for meds/fluids/bloods. Opioid appropriate for severe suspected fracture pain.
    
- **Discussion prompts:**
    
    - Monitor for opioid side effects; consider regional anaesthesia (they do next).

## Management

### “X-rays of his leg are taken (See x-rays) including a long leg femur view (not shown).”

- **Meaning:** you must image the **whole bone** (hip to knee to include femur) because pathology can be proximal/distal. Long view helps detect lesion extent and surgical planning.
    
- **Discussion prompts:**
    
    - In suspected pathological fracture, you often need staging imaging later too.
        

### “A femoral nerve block is performed in ED and the leg is immobilised in a Thomas Splint.”

- **Meaning:** excellent pain management + stabilisation.
    
    - **Femoral nerve block** = big reduction in pain, less opioid needed.
        
    - **Thomas splint** = immobilises femur, reduces movement, bleeding, pain.
        
- **Discussion prompts:**
    
    - What are risks? (local anaesthetic toxicity, nerve injury, infection—rare if done properly)
        

### “Following appropriate investigations including a biopsy of the bone lesion, an operation was performed to internally fix his left leg.”

- **Meaning:** the phrase “bone lesion” confirms this is not a routine fracture. Biopsy **before** definitive oncology planning is key: you don’t want to assume the diagnosis. Internal fixation is often required for function/pain.
    
- **Discussion prompts:**
    
    - Ortho-oncology principles: stabilise + get tissue diagnosis.
        

### “PSA was normal.”

- **Meaning:** reduces likelihood of prostate cancer as the source of bone metastasis (prostate commonly causes bone mets). Not definitive alone, but helpful clue.
    

### “A chest X-Ray was reported as having a radio opaque lesion at the right hilum.”

- **Meaning:** points toward a **lung mass**/hilar lesion—very relevant with heavy smoking history.
    

### “The biopsy of the site showed undifferentiated adenocarcinoma.”

- **Meaning:** confirms **malignancy**. Adenocarcinoma is consistent with a lung primary. “Undifferentiated” often suggests more aggressive behaviour.
    
- **Discussion prompts:**
    
    - Next steps are staging (CT chest/abdomen, PET, brain imaging) and MDT discussion.

## Neurological deterioration

### “During his post-operative recovery, Mr MacDonald became increasingly clumsy and developed a headache.”

- **Meaning:** headache + new neurological symptoms in a cancer patient = think **intracranial pathology** (metastasis/edema/bleed).
    
- **Discussion prompts:**
    
    - Urgent neuro exam + brain imaging indicated.
        

### “He then developed weakness which progressed over several days to paralysis of the right side of his face, right arm and right leg.”

- **Meaning:** progression over days suggests a space-occupying lesion with edema rather than sudden stroke (though stroke can’t be excluded without imaging). Right-sided involvement points to **left hemisphere** lesion.
    

### “The clinical examination at this time showed him to have exaggerated reflexes on the right-hand side, with a Babinski’s sign…”

- **Meaning:** classic **upper motor neuron (UMN) signs** → problem is in brain/spinal cord (not peripheral nerve).

- The Babinski sign (or reflex) is a neurological test where stroking the sole of the foot causes the big toe to move upward (dorsiflexion) and other toes to fan out. A positive Babinski sign in adults indicates an upper motor neuron lesion or damage to the central nervous system, such as a stroke, brain tumor, or multiple sclerosis.

### “…and an inability to voluntarily move his right arm, right leg and lower two thirds of his face.”

- **Meaning:**
    
    - Arm/leg UMN weakness fits left motor cortex/internal capsule involvement.
        
    - “Lower two-thirds of face” implies **UMN facial weakness** (forehead usually spared) → supports a central lesion.
        

### “Mr MacDonald had not lost consciousness.”

- **Meaning:** he’s still awake → not a global cerebral catastrophe; focal deficit more likely. Still urgent.

### Diagnosis (what disease is it?)

- **Most likely disease:** **Metastatic lung adenocarcinoma**
    
    - Evidence:
        
        - **Minor twist → severe pain + collapse** = low-energy mechanism suggests **pathological fracture**
            
        - **6 weeks progressive pain** before the “injury” = underlying bone pathology
            
        - Investigations show **bone lesion** + **biopsy = undifferentiated adenocarcinoma**
	        - Differentiated adenocarcinoma (well-differentiated) consists of cancer cells that closely resemble normal, healthy gland cells, indicating slow growth and lower aggression. In contrast, undifferentiated adenocarcinoma consists of highly abnormal, immature cells that bear no resemblance to normal tissue, making them aggressive, fast-growing, and associated with a poorer prognosis
            
        - **CXR: right hilar lesion** + heavy smoking history → lung primary likely
            
        - **PSA normal** → prostate less likely source of bone mets
            
- **Neurology later:** **Probable brain metastasis (left hemisphere)**
    
    - Headache + progressive right face/arm/leg weakness + **UMN signs (hyperreflexia, Babinski)**

## Management priorities (what to treat first)

### 1) Acute ED phase (fracture + pain)

- **Analgesia + IV access**
    
    - Cannula + **opioid analgesia**
        
- **Immobilise and reduce pain/bleeding**
    
    - **Femoral nerve block** + **Thomas splint**
        
- **Imaging**
    
    - X-rays including **long femur view**
        
- **Neurovascular checks**
    
    - Foot pulses present (reassuring)
        

### 2) Confirm pathology + stabilise limb

- **Biopsy of bone lesion** (don’t assume cause)
    
- **Surgical fixation** (internal fixation performed)
    
- **Post-op rehab**: physio/OT, mobilisation plan
    
- **VTE prevention** (cancer + surgery + immobility)
    

### 3) Treat metastatic bone disease (after fixation)

- **Radiotherapy to bone lesion** (pain + local control)
    
- **Bone-protective therapy** (e.g., bisphosphonate/denosumab) to reduce future skeletal events
    
- Ongoing **pain management** + functional support
    

### 4) Treat underlying lung cancer (systemic disease)

- **Staging + profiling**
    
    - CT/PET staging; molecular markers + PD-L1 (guides therapy)
        
- **Systemic treatment options**
    
    - **Targeted therapy** (if actionable mutation)
        
    - **Immunotherapy**
        
    - **Chemotherapy** (often platinum-based, depending on profile/fitness)
        
- **Early palliative care involvement** for symptoms + planning (not “end-of-life only”)
    

### 5) New neurological deterioration (urgent)

- **Urgent brain imaging** (new focal deficits + headache)
    
- Likely immediate supportive treatment:
    
    - **Steroids (e.g., dexamethasone)** for cerebral oedema
        
- Definitive options (depend on number/size/location):
    
    - **Stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS)** / **whole brain radiotherapy (WBRT)** / **neurosurgical resection**
        

---

## MDT roles (who does what)

- **ED:** pain control, immobilisation, initial imaging, safe pathway start
    
- **Orthopaedics:** fracture stabilisation + biopsy planning
    
- **Radiology:** fracture imaging + chest imaging + staging scans
    
- **Pathology:** confirms adenocarcinoma on biopsy
    
- **Oncology/Lung MDT:** staging, systemic therapy, radiotherapy coordination
    
- **Neuro/neurosurgery/radiation oncology:** manage suspected brain mets
    
- **Physio/OT:** mobilisation, function, home adaptations
    
- **Palliative care:** pain control, symptom support, goals-of-care conversations
